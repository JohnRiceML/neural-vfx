# Advect the detail, then teach the network

No published method synthesizes believable 3D smoke wisps with a neural network inside a few milliseconds. Every learned volumetric upsampler with measured timings (tempoGAN, Multi-Pass GAN, dictionary upsampling) takes **1–60 s per frame** on 1080 Ti / TITAN X-class GPUs, and diffusion super-resolution is slower still. The techniques that do ship real-time or near-real-time smoke are classical. They are wavelet/curl-noise upres keyed on the coarse sim's energy (Houdini Gas Upres, wavelet turbulence), texture coordinates advected with the coarse velocity (Unreal's Niagara Fluids Cine template, Neyret's advected textures), separate density and velocity resolutions (EmberGen, a 2026 LBM smoke paper), sparse brick pools (NVIDIA Flow), and temporally amortized, camera-reprojected ray marching (Nubis, Frostbite, FSR 2). Where real-time neural work does exist for smoke, it spends its milliseconds on **lighting, not geometry**: Neural Six-way Lightmaps reports 1.2 ms of inference. For this project, that means the wisp deficit should be fixed in object space with advected or procedural detail. This approach is view-consistent by construction, and it sidesteps the motion-vector mismatch the team measured. The learned stage should then be retargeted to what networks are demonstrably good at in this budget: adversarially trained refinement with a recurrent history, or predicting where and how strongly the procedural detail should appear. The team's own findings match industry practice. Camera-only reprojection is what production volumetrics use, warp-error flicker metrics are known to reward blur, and regression losses produce conditional-mean blur even when the training targets contain full fine-scale power.

**How to read the evidence tags.** Every number below carries one tag. **(P)** means it was read in the primary source during research. **(V)** means it is a vendor or author self-report that was not independently checked. **(S)** means a secondary write-up, forum post, review or anecdote. **(R)** means the researcher recalled it from a known primary source but did not re-verify it, so check it before quoting. **(E)** means it is an estimate or inference by the researchers or by this report, not a measurement. No source reported RTX 50-series timings for any smoke-detail technique, so every judgement about fit on the RTX 5080 is an extrapolation.

## Learned 3D upres costs 1–60 s per frame; procedural upres is what ships

### Volumetric GANs and dictionaries are three orders of magnitude too slow

tempoGAN is the standard baseline for learned smoke detail. It turns a 64^3 input into a 256^3 output in **17.9 s per frame on a GTX 1080 Ti (P)**. A 34^3→136^3 tile alone takes about 2.2 s (P). The 3D model took about 9 days to train on two GPUs, and the authors concede it "can also be slower than … calculating the advection for a high resolution grid" ([tempoGAN, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1801.09710)). Multi-Pass GAN replaces 3D convolutions with 2D slice passes and reaches **10.14 s at 256^3 and 59.65 s at 512^3 on a 1080 Ti (P)** ([Multi-Pass GAN, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.01689)). Bai et al.'s learned-dictionary upsampler takes 1–3 s for 50^3→200^3 on a TITAN X (P), compared with about 12 s for tempoGAN at similar size ([Bai et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.09166)). Scaling tempoGAN's 17.9 s by a generous 5–10x effective-throughput gain plus TensorRT still leaves **roughly 1–3 s per frame on an RTX 5080 (E)**. That is about 1,000x over the 2.5 ms detail budget. The team found that route (a) fits only at 25% occupancy or less with a small network, which is consistent with this. No 2021–2026 successor reporting real-time 3D smoke super-resolution turned up, but the search was not exhaustive.

These papers are still worth reading for their training design, not their architectures. tempoGAN's temporal discriminator sees frame triplets in which the neighbours are advected onto frame *t* using the low-res velocity. Its feature-space loss in the discriminator's own layers carries most of the detail, with L1 kept at a small weight. A plain L2 temporal loss was dropped because it "reduced overall density" (P). Noise fields supplied as inputs were ignored by the trained network, and vorticity inputs helped only subtly (P). Bai et al. make a point that bears directly on the team's data. They train on **independently simulated** coarse/fine pairs rather than downsampled fine data, because turbulent structure changes at coarser resolution (P). Their stated failure modes are missed short-lived vortices, output crisper than the real fine sim, and spurious wisps where the coarse sim has little smoke. Their own verdict is that tempoGAN "looks sharper but misses the fine dynamics" (P).

### Why the screen-space net sharpens edges but makes no wisps

Every published method that produced convincing filaments used a distribution-matching objective. The list is adversarial spatial plus temporal discriminators (tempoGAN, Multi-Pass GAN), learned atoms of real high-res structure (Bai), diffusion or flow-matching refinement, or exemplar-based style optimization. PDE-Refiner names the mechanism: MSE-trained surrogates neglect "non-dominant spatial frequency information," meaning the high-frequency, low-amplitude content ([PDE-Refiner, arXiv 2308.05732](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05732)) (P, abstract level). Fine turbulence is not determined by a 64^3 input, so an L1 objective learns the conditional mean, and the mean of many plausible wisp patterns is no wisp. Gradient and spectrum losses sharpen what is already there. A power-spectrum loss in particular matches energy but not phase or placement, which fits the observation of "sharpened edges without wisps" exactly (E).

The team's measurement rules out the most obvious alternative explanation. The research notes suggested that strong guiding might strip high frequencies from the 256^3 targets. Nielsen and Christensen showed that steady guiding "often leads to a lack of sufficiently high detail" and that time-dependent guiding lets more detail develop ([Nielsen & Christensen 2010](https://diglib.eg.org/items/7fde3794-e5a3-4cb8-a861-379cef4121d8)) (P, abstract). But the team measured that its steered sims keep 96–128% of the unsteered fine-scale power, so the data is not starved of energy. What it lacks is *alignment* between that energy and the 64^3 input above the coarse Nyquist frequency, and that is the loss's problem, not the data's. The in-progress adversarial run and the unconstrained-teacher-plus-distillation test both point the right way. An unconstrained teacher gives realistic detail statistics without spatial alignment, so it only makes sense with adversarial or feature-matching losses, as Bai's independently simulated pairs did (E).

### Procedural upres is cheap, production-proven, and perceptually competitive

Wavelet turbulence (Kim et al. 2008) uses the wavelet decomposition of a coarse sim to estimate where high-frequency energy is missing, then synthesizes curl-noise detail in a post-process with **no linear solve**. It ran in under 2 min per frame for a 720×576×576 output on an 8-core CPU in 2008 (P) ([Cornell WTURB](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~tedkim/WTURB)). Houdini's Gas Up Res productizes this. Its "Wavelets" source uses "the power of the finest detail coefficients used as a multiplier for turbulence" and "will only introduce turbulence where there existed some in the coarser smoke simulation". It also has a "Curl" source scaled by curl magnitude, an automatic frequency cutoff "that adds detail without changing the base sim's behavior", and a rest field that keeps the noise attached to the smoke (P) ([SideFX Gas Up Res](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/dop/gasupres.html)). EmberGen's upscaler reportedly upscales temperature and advected textures while keeping the original velocities. That account comes only from an informal developer forum comment (S) ([Reddit](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/Simulated/comments/echd0j/embergen_upscalingupresing_tests_great_realtime/fbbcwtx/?context=3)).

The strongest argument for procedural detail is perceptual. In ScalarFlow's 2AFC study against real captured smoke, **wavelet turbulence scored about on par with advection-reflection and slightly beat MacCormack** (P) ([ScalarFlow, arXiv 2011.10284](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.10284)). Nothing comparable exists for learned detail, and no study has separated the perceptual weight of turbulence, self-shadowing and flicker. A GPU port should cost a handful of compute dispatches with no solve, comfortably inside 2.5 ms, but no GPU timing was retrieved (E). The known weaknesses are generic-looking noise, "boiling", and no new large-scale structure.

### Learned corrections fix coarse motion, not sub-grid detail

Solver-in-the-loop training alternates network corrections with coarse solver steps, trains through a differentiable solver, and reports "stable rollouts of several hundred recurrent evaluation steps". The examples include 3D Navier-Stokes (P, abstract) ([Um et al., NeurIPS 2020](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2020/hash/43e4e6a6f341e00671e123714de019a8-Abstract.html)). Kochkov et al. match baselines run at **8–10x finer resolution, a 40–80x speedup, in 2D only** (P, abstract) ([arXiv 2102.01010](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01010)). A 2025 paper from the same group warns that direct corrections are "prone to instabilities caused by perturbations" (P) ([arXiv 2511.12764](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2511.12764)). For our pipeline, a correction network at 64^3 (262k cells) plausibly costs under 1 ms (E, unmeasured). It would keep vortices rolling up the way they do at 256^3, but it adds no wisps, and it needs a differentiable copy of the Warp solver. Deep Fluids is fast (up to 700x versus a CPU re-sim) but is tied to its training parameterization ([arXiv 1806.02071](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02071)) (P, abstract). That rules it out for open-ended gameplay.

## Shipping games bake or upres smoke; they rarely simulate it

### Engines treat 3D fluids as a hero tier and bake everything else

Unreal's Niagara Fluids 3D Gas is a dense grid solver with iterative pressure relaxation. Epic's own guidance says 3D sims are "best for hero effects or cinematics" and should be baked to flipbooks for real-time use (P) ([Epic, Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)). Epic publishes no ms costs. The only measured figure is a forum report: about **6 ms on an RTX 4080** for a cached 3D gas played back through Heterogeneous Volumes, with resolutions below the default of 200 judged too low in quality (S) ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/low-performance-with-cached-gas-simulations-niagara-fluids-3d-gas-sim/2177751)). A blog quotes 1.8 ms for 64^3 smoke and 2.4 ms for 64^3 fire, but gives no hardware or method, so treat it as unverified ([StraySpark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/niagara-fluids-for-gameplay-beyond-vfx-2026)). The Cine template already does a version of our detail stage. It **advects texture coordinates in a simulation stage and modulates the render with noise** (P). Heterogeneous Volumes is still Experimental. It ray-marches Sparse Volume Textures but uses a lighting cache that is dense even when the SVT is sparse, capped at 1024×1024×512 (P) ([Epic, Heterogeneous Volumes](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/heterogeneous-volumes-in-unreal-engine?lang=en-US)).

Unity ships no built-in 3D fluid solver. It offers six-way lightmapped smoke sprites in VFX Graph, with six baked directional lightmaps packed into two RGBA textures and blended by light direction (P) ([Unity six-way lighting](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.visualeffectgraph@17.2/manual/six-way-lighting.html)). In practice, almost all shipped smoke is flipbooks with six-way lighting and motion-vector frame blending. A smaller share is baked voxel data ray-marched at runtime: Guerrilla's Nubis^3 clouds were authored offline as NVDF voxels ([SIGGRAPH 2023 Advances](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2023/index.html)) (P, course listing). The rest is cheap gameplay propagation without Navier–Stokes. CS2's smoke is reconstructed by hobbyists as voxel flood fill plus ray marching, not documented by Valve ([GarrettGunnell recreation](https://github.com/GarrettGunnell/CS2-Smoke-Grenades)) (S). Our competition is therefore EmberGen-quality detail frozen into a flipbook, plus Niagara hero sims costing several ms. A live 64^3 effect that looks like a 200+ resolution sim at 6 ms or less would beat both on interactivity (E).

### Tools and middleware: high voxel counts, decoupled resolutions, and "AI" as branding

EmberGen is an authoring tool that happens to run in real time. Its output reaches games as flipbooks or VDBs. Version 1.0 added upscaling "to a billion voxels" ([CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/2023/03/jangafx-releases-embergen-1-0/)) (S). The 2.0 sparse solver claims about 200M active voxels on 6 GiB and 1B on 24 GiB, with a public beta labelled Q4 2026 (V) ([JangaFX roadmap](https://jangafx.com/roadmap/embergen)). Its demo shows 1B voxels "interactively" on an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell with no fps given (V) ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/upcoming-embergen-s-sparse-solver-handles-one-billion-voxels-in-real-time)). The roadmap's own table implies about 5x more capacity, not the claimed 10–15x. The lesson that transfers is the separation of smoke resolution from velocity resolution, for example 2x smoke over 1x velocity. Zibra sells "AI-powered" Smoke & Fire for Unity, but its one smoke cost figure is an anecdotal "about 2 ms" for a full-screen effect with no hardware stated (V) ([Zibra case study](https://www.zibra.ai/case-studies/enhancing-game-visuals-with-zibra-liquid-and-zibra-smoke-fire-a-creators-journey)). Its FAQ says ZibraVDB uses no neural networks (S, via search summary). ZibraVDB gives about 40x compression at default quality and decompresses at about **0.02–0.03 ms per million voxels on an RTX 3090 (V)** ([Zibra perf blog](https://www.zibra.ai/blog-posts/zibravdb-performance-and-quality-optimization)). At that rate, a 256^3 field costs about 0.4 ms just to decode (E). A system with a genuinely learned stage and published per-pass ms numbers would be unusual in this market.

### Sparse structures pay off for detail volumes, not a 64^3 solver

NVIDIA Flow is the canonical real-time sparse smoke and fire solver. It allocates **32×16×16 voxel blocks** (optionally 16×8×8) dynamically from emitters and simulation feedback, has no bounding box and a capped block pool, runs in HLSL on DX12/Vulkan, includes a combustion model, and exports NanoVDB (P) ([NVIDIA Flow docs](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/flow/index.html)). The block size comes from Flow 1's 64 KB tiled-resource tiles (P) ([FLOWUe4 intro](https://docs.nvidia.com/gameworks/content/artisttools/Flow/FLOWUe4_Intro.html)). No timings are published. NanoVDB is the read-mostly GPU VDB ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3450623.3464653)). NeuralVDB compresses 10–100x beyond it but explicitly does not target fast random access, and a follow-up recommends decoding to regular VDB before rendering (P) ([arXiv 2208.04448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.04448)). fVDB adds an IndexGrid and tensor-core sparse convolution ([NVIDIA fVDB](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/fVDB/)). The whole 64^3 domain is only 0.26M cells, so sparsity buys little in the solver. It matters for a 128^3–256^3 detail or density field and for several effects sharing one pool. A Flow-style brick pool with a page table fits a per-frame solver better than a deep VDB tree (E).

## Game reconstructors treat smoke as "reactive" and reproject by camera only

### Every production upscaler distrusts history on alpha-blended media

FSR 2, DLSS, XeSS and UE's TSR all depend on per-pixel motion vectors from opaque geometry plus history rectification. **None of them has a real solution for smoke**. What they document is a way to trust history less. AMD's FSR 2 reactive mask exists for content that does "not leave a footprint in the depth buffer or include motion vectors", such as "particles, or alpha-blended objects". AMD's guidance is to write alpha into the mask, clamped to about 0.9. The transparency-and-composition mask removes history locks (P) ([AMD FSR2 docs](https://gpuopen.com/manuals/fidelityfx_sdk/fidelityfx_sdk-page_techniques_super-resolution-temporal/)). DLSS's equivalent "bias current colour" mask is reportedly ignored by the current transformer model, according to a third-party issue that quotes guide 310.4.0 (S, unconfirmed) ([forge-engine issue](https://github.com/Eolan/forge-engine/issues/360)). UE TSR offers translucency velocity output and "Responsive AA" (R) ([UE TSR docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/temporal-super-resolution-in-unreal-engine)). The team found that fluid-velocity warping is worse than no warp, and these vendors reached the same conclusion: wrong reprojection is worse than none.

The cost of analytic temporal upscaling is well documented. FSR 2.3.3 at **2560×1440 output costs 0.3 ms on an RX 7900 XTX and 0.9 ms on an RX 6700 XT (P)**. ML upscalers cost more: on an RTX 4090 at 4K Performance, DLSS 3.7 CNN takes 0.51 ms, the DLSS 4 transformer 1.06 ms, and DLSS 4.5 preset M 1.39 ms (S, one blog measurement) ([Opinali](https://medium.com/@opinali/dlss-strikes-again-transformer-v2-04e44e63d5fc)). These upscalers stay within budget by keeping **one recurrent history buffer** and putting most of the compute at render resolution. Multi-frame stacks are too slow. STSS, a 417k-parameter joint supersampling and extrapolation network, takes 4.35 ms at 540p→1080p on an RTX 3090 in FP16 (P) ([arXiv 2312.10890](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10890v1)). Xiao et al.'s 2020 five-frame network is recalled at 18–25 ms (R) ([FB Research](https://research.facebook.com/publications/neural-supersampling-for-real-time-rendering/)).

### Production volumetrics reproject by camera at a representative depth

Volumetric fog and cloud renderers reproject by camera motion only. Froxel fog jitters samples along each froxel and blends with camera-reprojected history (S) ([Strand, volumetric fog](https://www.mattiasstrand.com/posts/volumetric-fog/)). Wronski's AC4 froxels and Hillaire's Frostbite volumetrics use an exponential history of about 0.9–0.95 (R) ([Frostbite 2015](https://www.ea.com/frostbite/news/physically-based-unified-volumetric-rendering-in-frostbite)). Nubis updates **1 pixel in 16 per frame** and cost about **2 ms for the whole sky on PS4** (S, secondary write-ups; check against the slides) ([bevy issue #18151](https://github.com/bevyengine/bevy/issues/18151)). Cloud renderers treat the ray-marched result as quasi-static between frames, which is exactly the quasi-steady plume behaviour the team observed. So the "apparent motion" of a smoke pixel should come from camera motion at the transmittance-weighted depth. Our renderer already outputs an opacity-weighted mean depth in `aux`. Remaining shape change should then be handled with a learned or appearance-based confidence, not by warping with gas velocity (E). If camera-only is not enough, there are two further options. One is optical flow on the low-res rendered smoke, which is how DLSS 3 and FSR 3 frame generation handle content whose motion vectors miss shading-driven motion ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/article/2747-amd-fsr-3-tech/)) (S). The other is the velocity of density iso-surfaces (∂ρ/∂t), which is near zero for a steady plume (E).

The same reprojection buys a cheap quality gain before any network runs. The recipe is to ray-march at half or quarter resolution with a per-frame jittered ray start, accumulate camera-reprojected history, clamp it against the current YCoCg neighbourhood, and upsample with depth- and alpha-aware joint bilateral weights. FSR-style clamps were designed for opaque surfaces and will over-reject history on low-contrast semi-transparent media, so the clamp needs to be **transmittance-aware** (E).

### Flicker metrics that reward blur are a known trap with a known fix

TecoGAN showed that frame-difference and warping-error metrics are minimized by blurry output. It proposed tOF and tLP, which compare the temporal change in optical flow or LPIPS against the *ground truth's* temporal change, and it trained with a spatio-temporal discriminator and a "Ping-Pong" loss (R) ([TecoGAN, arXiv 1811.09393](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09393)). Pairing a tLP/tOF-style score with a high-frequency-energy-relative-to-reference score penalizes blur in both space and time (E). Kernel-prediction networks (KPCN-style), which output per-pixel filter weights, are a robust way to limit hallucinated flicker. But they cannot add frequencies absent from their inputs, so they suit the stabilization part of the job, not the wisps (E). Our ray-march is deterministic, so neural denoisers (OIDN, DLSS Ray Reconstruction, ReSTIR volumes) matter only if the renderer moves to stochastic, few-step marching. In that case temporal accumulation is the cheap denoiser.

## Launch overhead, not FLOPs, limits both the solver and the network

### Runtime choice gives 1.5–2x, not 5x

The only hard in-engine number found is NVIDIA's. TensorRT for RTX inside Unreal NNE (RDG path) ran a style-transfer network in **3.8 ms versus 5.7 ms with DirectML at 1080p on an RTX 5090** (V). The same post warns that tiling small models across a framebuffer causes "frequent CUDA-graphics context switches" (V) ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/speed-up-unreal-engine-nne-inference-with-nvidia-tensorrt-for-rtx-runtime/)). The Fab listing claims 1.5–2x over DirectML (V). Our ~730k-parameter screen-space CNN already uses CUDA graphs, so TensorRT FP16 with fused conv+bias+activation should land around **0.8–1.4 ms, not 0.3 ms (E)**. The bigger wins come from the model itself. Structural re-parameterization (RepVGG, ECBSR) trains multi-branch blocks and exports plain 3×3 convolutions, so the extra branches cost nothing at inference (R) ([RepVGG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03697)). Pixel-unshuffling the half-res bounding box by 2, then distilling to 200k parameters or fewer, follows the recipe of efficient-SR challenge entries (E). For calibration, the NTIRE 2024 efficient-SR baseline RLFN has 0.317M parameters and runs in **13.54 ms on an RTX 3090** at challenge resolutions for ×4 SR (P) ([NTIRE 2024 ESR report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10343)). Production-budget networks therefore have to be much smaller or work mostly at low resolution. FP8 and INT8 risk banding in smooth density gradients, so standard practice is QAT, with the first and last layers kept in FP16 (R).

Cooperative vectors (D3D12 Shader Model 6.9, and VK_NV_cooperative_vector on Vulkan) expose tensor-core matrix-vector operations inside shaders, which removes the CUDA↔D3D12 crossing entirely (P) ([Microsoft DirectX GDC 2025](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/directx/announcing-directx-raytracing-1-2-pix-neural-rendering-and-more-at-gdc-2025/)). Intel claims a 10x inference speedup (V). Third-party NTC measurements conflict on the gain without cooperative vectors, from 1,500→650 fps to 1,500→1,000 fps on an RTX 4090 (S) ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/nvidias-new-tech-reduces-vram-usage-by-up-to-96-percent-in-beta-demo-rtx-neural-texture-compression-looks-impressive)). In-shader MLPs are practical once per pixel. A ray-marcher, however, takes 32–128 samples per pixel. At 1440p with 64 samples that is about 236M MLP evaluations per frame, likely several ms even on tensor cores (E). Per-sample neural detail is only viable if the MLP is evaluated on a cached, advected brick grid and then sampled trilinearly. Sparse convolution libraries (TorchSparse++ is 1.7–3.3x faster than its peers on an A100 (P), [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.12862)) are tuned for point clouds with well under 5% occupancy. At 64^3 with 5–30% occupancy, dense FP16 convolution over occupied 8^3 or 16^3 bricks with halos is the practical middle ground (E). No benchmark covers this regime.

### The 64^3 pressure solve is latency-bound; fuse it and cap the V-cycles

Our solver spends 75–88% of a 0.8–1.0 ms step in MGPCG, with about 25 dependent launches per iteration. A 64^3 FP32 field is 1 MB, which an RTX 5080 streams in about a microsecond. Launch latency and tails of roughly 2–5 µs each therefore dominate (E). The EmberGen author's notes give the production rule of thumb: one V-cycle is "equivalent to about a thousand Jacobi iterations" at the cost of about 10, 1 V-cycle already gives "decent results", and Jacobi at 40–160 iterations still smears and gains volume (S, author's gist; no GPU or per-cycle timings) ([vassvik gist](https://gist.github.com/vassvik/f06a453c18eae03a9ad4dc8cc011d2dc)). The concrete levers are these. Capture a fixed-iteration solve in a CUDA graph (`wp.ScopedCapture`). Fuse smoother, residual and restriction into one kernel per level. Solve levels of 16^3 and below in a single shared-memory thread block. Fuse the CG reductions. Warm-start from the previous frame's pressure. Consider 1–2 V-cycles as the whole solver, without the CG wrapper. The researchers estimate **2–4x on the solve, from about 0.7 ms to 0.2–0.35 ms (E, unmeasured)**. That would free budget for a density field at higher resolution than velocity.

Learned pressure solvers do not help at this size. Lan et al.'s neural preconditioner beats AMGX by about 3x and CG by about 12x, but it still takes **0.072 (units unlabeled, likely seconds) at 128^3 on an RTX 3080 (P)**, and 79.4% of that time is network evaluation. In the same paper, a FluidNet-style one-shot CNN projection leaves a residual of 2.035, so it does not really converge (P) ([arXiv 2310.00177](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.00177v5)). The best 2026 result beats a highly tuned geometric multigrid by only **1.03–1.26x at 16–64M unknowns (V, abstract)** ([ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10015904)). At 64^3, network launch costs would swamp any gain (E). Lattice Boltzmann avoids a global solve: 157.3 fps at 64×64×128, hardware not captured (S) ([Visual Computer 2018](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00371-018-1514-x)). But it moves 19–27 distributions per cell, so it is not obviously cheaper at our size (E).

## Advected carriers beat Gaussians, neural fields and generative video

### Lagrangian and advected detail is the mature, view-consistent route

Pfaff et al.'s anisotropic turbulence particles offload small-scale detail to particles driven by a k-ε model, so that "a very low grid resolution" is enough for the base flow. The paper claims "millions of particles at high framerates" on GPUs (P, abstract; no frame times found) ([NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2010-12_scalable-fluid-simulation-using-anisotropic-turbulence-particles)). Neyret's advected textures blend layers of periodically regenerated, advected texture parameterizations, weighted by accumulated deformation ([EG DL](https://diglib.eg.org/items/c4bf80cc-23f0-4107-958d-e03c0b9c8ac4/full)). Lagrangian Texture Advection preserves the texture's Fourier spectrum in real time on very large scenes (S, abstract via search summary) ([core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/93038924.pdf)). For us, the cheapest form is a 64^3 or 128^3 advected noise-coordinate field with two-phase regeneration, sampled during the march. It costs one or two extra 3D texture taps per step plus one advection pass, **probably well under 1 ms (E)**. Because the detail moves with the gas and the screen only shows the integral, it is temporally stable and view-consistent, and it never needs a screen-space motion vector. Flow-map methods (Neural Flow Maps, Particle Flow Maps, Vortex Particle Flow Maps) preserve vorticity remarkably well. VPFM keeps a 2D leapfrog stable for more than 600 s (P) ([arXiv 2505.21946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21946)). But these are offline research simulators with no published per-step timings. Their realistic contribution is cheaper ideas for the solver stage, such as BFECC-style back-and-forth advection.

### Gaussian splats are a data structure for tracers, not a renderer we can afford

All the smoke Gaussian work found reconstructs smoke from multi-view video offline. The idea worth borrowing is from LagrangianSplats (SIGGRAPH 2026): Gaussians are **passive tracers advected by a divergence-free velocity field**, not free variables. A review calls it "not yet capable of real-time reconstruction", and no fps is published (S) ([project](https://taoningxiao.github.io/lagrangiansplats/)). Rendering is the obstacle. Rasterized 3DGS sorts by mean once per frame and pops on overlapping semi-transparent media. EVER's exact ray-traced ellipsoids fix this but run at **about 30 fps at 720p on an RTX 4090 (P)** ([EVER, arXiv 2410.01804](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01804v5)), roughly 10x over our budget at 1440p (E). "Don't Splat your Gaussians" provides closed-form transmittance for emissive and scattering kernel primitives (a cloud in 5.6k primitives and 224 KB, versus 4 GB as 1024^3 voxels), but only in Mitsuba (P) ([arXiv 2405.15425](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15425v2)). The workable compromise is to splat advected detail primitives into a froxel or low-res 3D texture that the existing march already samples, which needs no sort (E). No paper uses Gaussians as a real-time sub-grid layer driven by a live sim. That is an open niche, not established practice.

### Neural volumes, compressed playback and generative video stay offline

Neural smoke fields are reconstruction tools. PINF's "Game" scene took **64 hours on one Quadro RTX 8000 (P)** ([arXiv 2206.06577](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06577)). NeuralVDB takes about 3 min to decode the Disney Cloud (P). For hero effects that can be authored ahead of time, ZibraVDB-class compressed playback avoids hallucination altogether, but it gives up interactivity (V numbers only). Generative video refinement of coarse sims is validated in concept and only offline. WonderPlay calls its coarse smoke "too sticky" from numerical viscosity and refines it with a video model ([arXiv 2505.18151](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18151)). FluidNexus and SmokeSVD do the same ([SmokeSVD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12156)). One-step diffusion SR is recalled at 100 ms or more per 512² image on data-centre GPUs (R) ([OSEDiff](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08177)), and one 3D flow paper states that cost has confined diffusion SR to low-resolution 3D settings (P) ([arXiv 2510.19971](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.19971)). No "baked library plus live-sim-conditioned blending" system has been published.

The most game-relevant neural smoke result is about lighting. **Real-time Neural Six-way Lightmaps** (arXiv 2604.03748, 2026) works in three steps: a coarse large-step march builds a 512² guiding map in about 2 ms, a U-Net predicts six-way lightmaps plus transparency and emission in **1.2 ms with TensorRT (P; another section says 3 ms)**, and the final render takes under 0.3 ms. It ran in Unreal on an unnamed 24 GB GPU with 16,384 cores, consistent with an RTX 4090. Its own bottleneck is the simulation, with 400^3 density advection at 11.5 ms (P) ([arXiv 2604.03748](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03748)). Temporal stability comes from a flow loss and is not measured. For fire, which is emission-dominated, the notes found no neural real-time work. The cheaper target there is advected noise along the flame front plus a correct blackbody mapping (E).

| Representation | Published speed (hardware) | Real-time? | Slot | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Advected noise coordinates / textures | Real time (S); <1 ms for us (E) | Yes | Sampled in the march | Stretching; needs regeneration |
| Turbulence / vortex particles (Pfaff) | "Millions of particles at high framerates" (P, abstract) | Likely | Sub-grid velocity or density on top of 64^3 | No modern timings |

## Five techniques worth our milliseconds, ranked

The ranking weighs four things: published evidence that the technique produces believable detail, fit inside the 6 ms split, temporal stability without fluid-velocity motion vectors, and how much existing code it reuses. Every cost on the RTX 5080 is an estimate until the team measures it.

| Rank | Technique | Budget slot | Expected cost (E) | Why it ranks here | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Object-space advected detail keyed on coarse energy**: curl/wavelet noise whose amplitude follows sub-grid energy (Houdini "Wavelets"/"Curl"), carried by advected noise coordinates with two-phase regeneration (Neyret, Niagara Cine template), sampled in the ray-march | Detail (2.5 ms) | <1 ms | Production-proven, matches real smoke perceptually in ScalarFlow, view-consistent, makes the motion-vector problem disappear | Generic or boiling look; needs art tuning |
| 2 | **Temporally amortized half-res march**: jittered starts, camera-only reprojection at the opacity-weighted depth, transmittance-aware clamp, joint-bilateral upsample | Render (1.5 ms) | Same or less than now, plus ~0.3 ms resolve | Standard Nubis/Frostbite/FSR recipe; gives a stable base and frees march steps | Over-rejection on low-contrast smoke |
| 3 | **Retargeted learned stage**: a small re-parameterized, distilled recurrent net trained with adversarial patch, feature-matching and tempoGAN-style temporal discriminators on camera-warped triplets, with an appearance-based confidence. Its first job is to modulate procedural detail parameters (amplitude, scale, anisotropy) rather than invent pixels | Detail (remaining ~1–1.5 ms) | 0.8–1.4 ms with TensorRT FP16, less after distillation | The only objective family that has ever produced filaments; conditioning procedural detail is lower-risk than synthesizing it | GAN instability; hallucinated flicker |
| 4 | **Fused, graph-captured multigrid plus decoupled density resolution**: 1–2 fixed V-cycles in a CUDA graph, then reinvest the freed time in a 128^3 density/temperature field advected by 64^3 velocity | Sim (2.0 ms) | Solve 0.7 → 0.2–0.35 ms | Pure engineering gain; EmberGen and the 2026 LBM paper both decouple density from velocity | 256^3 density advection could be expensive (that paper took 11.5 ms at 400^3) |
| 5 | **Solver-in-the-loop correction at 64^3** | Sim | <1 ms (unmeasured) | Only evidenced way to make 64^3 large-scale motion behave like 256^3; complements the detail stage | Needs a differentiable solver; instability under perturbation |

**Try #1 first.** It is the cheapest experiment and directly targets the observed failure (no wisps). It needs no training data, it is stable frame to frame by construction, and it also becomes the baseline any neural stage must beat at equal ms, scored with tLP/tOF-style metrics rather than raw warp error. Run #4's CUDA-graph capture in parallel, because it is mechanical work that buys back budget for everything else. Then take the adversarial screen-space run that is already in progress and feed it the advected-detail render as input. That turns its job from "invent wisps" into "correct and place wisps". Neural lighting in the style of Neural Six-way Lightmaps is the strongest candidate just outside the top five. It is evidenced at 1.2 ms and may buy more perceived quality per ms than geometric detail, but no study has separated the two.

**Stop doing these.** Stop warping history with fluid-velocity motion vectors; use camera-only reprojection at the representative depth. Stop using frame-difference or warp-error flicker as an acceptance gate on its own. Stop adding sharpening terms (gradient, spectrum) and expecting wisps, since the targets already carry the power and the loss is what discards it. Stop pursuing a dense 4x volumetric GAN to 256^3 (route A at full occupancy), diffusion or multi-frame SR inside the budget, exact Gaussian-splat rendering, learned pressure preconditioners at 64^3, and sparse-convolution libraries at 5–30% occupancy. Every one of these has published or estimated costs an order of magnitude or more over its slot, or gains under 1.3x.

## Conclusion

The literature does not support the framing of a "neural detail stage" as a network that generates turbulence. In real time, networks have so far earned their place by deciding how much to trust history (DLSS, FSR's learned successors), by predicting lighting (neural six-way lightmaps), or by correcting a solver's large-scale behaviour. The fine structure itself, in every fast system found, comes from transport: noise, textures or particles carried by the coarse velocity. The team's two negative findings (velocity warping fails, flicker metrics reward blur) are the same lessons the upscaler vendors and the TecoGAN authors reached. That suggests the remaining open problem is narrower than it looked. A learned model only has to decide *where* and *how strongly* advected detail should appear, and it can be trained with adversarial and temporal discriminators that this budget can afford.

Two evidence gaps should shape the next experiments more than any paper does. No published study separates the perceptual value of fine wisps from that of self-shadowing and temporal stability for real-time smoke. And no RTX 50-series timings exist for any of these techniques. The team's own per-pass measurements of advected detail versus the adversarial net, at equal milliseconds and judged against unsteered 256^3 renders, would be new data rather than a re-run of prior work.
