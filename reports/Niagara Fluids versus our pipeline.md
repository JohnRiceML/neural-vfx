# Our solver leads, Unreal's engine wins

On the simulation itself, our pipeline is probably ahead of Unreal's Niagara Fluids 3D gas. We use a stronger pressure solve, a real fuel/combustion model and sim-carried detail at a finer resolution than the velocity grid. We also spend about 2 ms of GPU per frame where the only measured Niagara figure is about 6 ms for rendering alone. On everything around the simulation we are far behind: scene lighting, collision with real geometry, caching, level of detail, artist tools, platform coverage and a renderer that composites with the rest of a game frame. **No like-for-like measurement exists.** Epic publishes no millisecond timings for 3D gas. The few community numbers use different resolutions, GPUs and engine versions. Our own numbers come from a Python sandbox running one effect outside any engine, and our quality has only ever been scored against our own 256^3 reference, never against Niagara. Epic itself positions live 3D gas as a "hero effects or cinematics" tool and tells game teams to bake. That leaves live 3D smoke in games largely empty: no confirmed shipped game runs it. A small, tightly specified head-to-head test in Unreal on our RTX 5080 is worth doing. It is the only way to turn "probably ahead" into a number, and it would show which integration gaps actually cost frame time.

**How to read the reliability tags.** **[Epic]** means Epic documentation or release notes. **[Epic staff]** means a named Epic employee in a forum or interview. **[Community]** means forum users, code readings by third parties, or academic users, and is hearsay relative to Epic. **[Low-reliability]** means a commercial blog with no stated method, or one that contradicts Epic. **[Ours]** means measured in our own sandbox: Python, idle RTX 5080, one effect, no engine. **[Inference]** means this report's reasoning, not a measurement.

## Both are dense Eulerian grids, but they spend their budget differently

Niagara 3D gas and our solver belong to the same family: a dense, fixed, uniform box of cells on the GPU, advecting density, temperature and velocity, adding buoyancy and vorticity confinement, then projecting to remove divergence. Niagara sizes the grid with one "Resolution Max Axis" over a world-space box, with per-face open or solid boundaries **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)). No Epic source describes a sparse, adaptive or camera-following simulation grid. Sparse Volume Textures appear only for caching and rendering **[Epic]** ([Unreal Fest 2023 talk listing](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/talks-and-demos-creating-visual-effects-with-niagara-fluids-sparse-volume-textures-and-heterogeneous-volumes-in-ue-unreal-fest-2023/1683078)). The memory scaling is therefore the same kind as ours.

The bets differ. Epic's quick start sets **Resolution Max Axis to 300 over a 300 x 300 x 600 box** for an explosion **[Epic]** ([Quick Start Guide](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-quick-start-guide-for-unreal-engine)), which works out to about 3.4M cells. A user reports the smoke template default as 200 and says quality collapses below it **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/low-performance-with-cached-gas-simulations-niagara-fluids-3d-gas-sim/2177751)). That is roughly 13x our 64^3 velocity grid (262k cells) and 1.6x our optional 128^3 scalar grid **[Inference]**. Niagara puts its budget into one high-resolution grid with a cheap solver. We use a small velocity grid with a strong solver and carry finer detail on top of it.

The solver gap is the clearest difference, and also the least well sourced. Epic documents only "Pressure Solve Iterations" and a "Pressure Relaxation" value between 0 and 1, recommended "close to 1" **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)). A user analysis says the pressure is "solved using an iterative Jacobi method" and gets better results at 10 iterations than the 6 suggested in Epic videos **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/niagara-fluids-injecting-scalar-fields-from-external-data-sources/740604)). A 2022 code reading of the *liquid* template found **red-black SOR at 50 iterations** on collocated, cell-centred velocity with central-difference divergence and gradient, not a staggered MAC grid **[Community, liquid not gas]** ([songjihu.top code reading](http://songjihu.top/2022/05/13/NiagaraFluids%E4%BB%A3%E7%A0%81%E5%88%86%E6%9E%901/)). No source mentions multigrid or conjugate gradient anywhere in Niagara Fluids. If gas uses the same modules, which is plausible but unverified, a fixed 6-50 relaxation sweeps on a 200-300 cell axis leaves large-scale divergence largely unresolved, and collocated stencils invite checkerboard pressure modes **[Inference]**. Our multigrid-preconditioned CG, or the fused V-cycle, removes low-frequency divergence in a few iterations **[Ours]**. That makes our smoke more incompressible per frame at large scales, at the cost of far fewer cells.

No public source names Niagara's advection scheme. Semi-Lagrangian is the likely default, but that is unconfirmed. Nothing found mentions MacCormack or BFECC, which we use **[Ours]**.

Fire shows the same pattern. Niagara exposes no fuel, ignition or burn parameters. Fire is a temperature scalar with buoyancy and exponential dissipation, mapped to colour by a Black Body or Curve render mode **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine); [Quick Start Guide](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-quick-start-guide-for-unreal-engine)). A community analysis calls it "not a complete thermo-fluid-dynamic model", with no diffusion of density or temperature, only decay **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/niagara-fluids-injecting-scalar-fields-from-external-data-sources/740604)). Our fuel/combustion model and heat diffusion are more physically structured **[Ours]**. That is a modelling advantage, not a visual one. Our fire is still grainy, with short flames in the particle modes, so the better model has not yet produced better-looking fire.

For detail, Niagara uses vorticity confinement, Perlin-style turbulence injected in density and temperature bands, and, in the Cine template only, texture coordinates advected through the sim **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)). Our advected noise coordinates are the same idea as the Cine template, so there we are matching Epic, not leading. We found no Niagara equivalent of our 128^3 scalar field driven by 64^3 velocity, or of our 1M particles deposited into a 128^3 render volume. Niagara's particle advection is one-way, for secondary particles **[Epic staff]** ([Inside Unreal thread](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/inside-unreal-creating-fluid-simulation-in-ue5/533695?page=2)). Not finding a feature in the docs does not prove it is absent.

## No like-for-like timings exist, and the few numbers point at rendering

**Epic has published no GPU timings for 3D gas** at any resolution or on any GPU. Its guidance is qualitative: 3D has "higher memory and GPU cost", 2D is "better suited for games and real-time use", and 3D results can be baked to flipbooks **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)).

The one first-hand measurement is a community report. A *cached* 3D gas smoke at resolution 200, played back in an empty scene on an **RTX 4080, cost about 6 ms under the HeterogeneousVolumes GPU stat**, and the frame rate fell from about 110 to about 50 fps. The UE version and screen resolution were not stated **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/low-performance-with-cached-gas-simulations-niagara-fluids-3d-gas-sim/2177751)). That number is render only. Caching removed the solver, and the ray-march alone cost about 6 ms. A forum reply called the ray-march "extremely expensive on its own" **[Community]**. EmberGen shows the same pattern: on an RTX 3090 a high-resolution scene took 13.5 ms to simulate but 150-200 ms to render **[Community]** ([realtimevfx thread](https://realtimevfx.com/t/embergen-alpha-real-time-volumetric-fluid-simulations-for-vfx-artists/9915)). For volumetric smoke, rendering, not simulation, tends to set the bill.

The other numbers are weaker. A studio blog lists **3D smoke 64^3 at 1.8 ms, 128^3 at 5.5 ms and 3D fire 64^3 at 2.4 ms on PS5/Xbox Series X, "measured on UE5.7.1"**. It does not say whether that covers sim, render or both, and gives no scene, capture tool or run count **[Low-reliability]** ([StraySpark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/niagara-fluids-for-gameplay-beyond-vfx-2026)). The same blog's dates for when the plugin went "production-ready" contradict each other and contradict Epic's own Beta label. Users also report that 3D gas "runs very smoothly in UE5.3, but becomes nearly unusable in 5.5" in the editor **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/ue5-5-niagara-gas-simulation-are-unbearably-slow-in-editor/2439193)), and that the grid crashes at high resolution **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/how-to-fix-crashing-with-niagara-grid3d-gas-master-emitter-resolution-too-high/2087476/3)). No published figure gives VRAM per simulation.

Against that, our numbers look strong but are not comparable. **[Ours]** The 64^3 sim step costs 0.88-1.01 ms by default and 0.48-0.62 ms with the fused solve. The ray-march at 2560x1440 costs 0.74 ms plain and 1.08 ms with advected noise. The best mode totals about 2.0 ms GPU per frame, estimated at about 2.2 ms at full 1440p but not measured as one run. Our 0.74-1.08 ms render is several times cheaper than the 4080's ~6 ms. But that report used a 200-resolution volume where we use 64^3 or 128^3, it ran an engine renderer with a lighting cache and scene compositing, and its screen size is unknown. If the StraySpark 64^3 figure of 1.8 ms on a PS5 were real, it would favour Niagara on equal hardware, because an RTX 5080 is far faster than a PS5 **[Inference]**. The source is too weak to rely on. The honest summary is that we have a plausible cost lead with no controlled evidence behind it. Raising our own grid would not close the resolution gap cheaply either: a direct 256^3 sim step costs 43 ms **[Ours]**.

## Niagara's renderer does more, and Epic still labels it Experimental

Niagara 3D gas has two render paths. The first is the template's own ray-march material, with Render Density and Render Temperature modes, Shadow Quality and Shadow Max Steps, and a step-size multiplier **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)). The second is Heterogeneous Volumes, a renderer that marches world-space and frustum-aligned voxel grids. It adds a viewport downsample cvar, a step cap of 512 by default, and a dense lighting cache of at most 1024 x 1024 x 512. Its experimental options are shadow casting onto the scene, indirect lighting that is off by default and meant for "high-end PCs and Cinematic scalability settings", a translucency composite option, and path tracer support **[Epic]** ([Heterogeneous Volumes docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/heterogeneous-volumes-in-unreal-engine?lang=en-US)). The current docs still mark Heterogeneous Volumes **Experimental**, although the 5.5 release notes called it Beta **[Epic]** ([UE 5.5 Release Notes](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/unreal-engine-5-5-release-notes)). MegaLights does not support it **[Epic]** ([MegaLights docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/megalights-in-unreal-engine)).

The template path samples up to two lights connected as data interfaces **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids Reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-reference-in-unreal-engine)). That is one light more than our single shadow volume, and it reads real game lights where we use a fixed one. Users report many problems with this renderer: separate volumes that do not sort against each other, depth sorting "still unsolved" in 5.4 Preview 1, shadow instability, and black sims fixed only by disabling the lighting cache **[Community]** ([forum thread](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/5-3-heterogeneous-volume-rendering-tips-and-tricks/1291002?page=3); [realtimevfx](https://realtimevfx.com/t/niagara-gas-sim-appears-partially-completely-black/30909)). In 2022 Epic staff said fluids could not be shadowed by scene geometry **[Epic staff]** ([Inside Unreal thread](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/inside-unreal-creating-fluid-simulation-in-ue5/533695?page=2)).

Feature for feature, our half-resolution march with one-light shadow and blackbody fire resembles the template path. It lacks the composite, scene shadows, second light, indirect lighting and path-tracer parity **[Inference]**. Our renderer is fast partly because it does less. An honest comparison has to switch off Niagara's extras, or price them separately.

## Integration and production use are where we are furthest behind

Niagara's main lead is everything around the solver. Colliders come from tagged actors through data interfaces for static meshes, geometry collections and physics assets for characters **[Epic]** ([Quick Start Guide](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-quick-start-guide-for-unreal-engine)). An Epic artist also names per-mesh and global distance fields, landscape and skeletal mesh **[Epic staff]** ([80.lv interview](https://80.lv/articles/working-with-niagara-fluids-to-create-water-simulations)). A code reading shows solid velocity entering the pressure solve, so moving colliders push the gas **[Community]** ([songjihu.top](http://songjihu.top/2022/05/13/NiagaraFluids%E4%BB%A3%E7%A0%81%E5%88%86%E6%9E%901/)). Any Niagara particle emitter can inject density, temperature and velocity **[Epic]**. Artists get grouped no-code templates, debug slices, a Flipbook Baker, Niagara Sim Cache playback from Blueprint, and VDB import into Sparse Volume Textures **[Epic]** ([Niagara Fluids docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/niagara-fluids-in-unreal-engine); [HV docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/heterogeneous-volumes-in-unreal-engine?lang=en-US)). Scalability overrides apply at Cinematic quality by default **[Epic]**.

We have none of this. Our obstacles are simple spheres and boxes, emitters are driven by the mouse, and we have no game lights, LOD, caching, artist controls, multi-effect tests or tests on weaker GPUs and consoles **[Ours]**. A native D3D12 runtime exists on a separate branch, but it lacks the detail modes and is unverified **[Ours]**.

Niagara's maturity and reach should not be overstated either. Epic's current (5.9) docs still say "this plugin is still in Beta" and "use caution when shipping with it" **[Epic]** ([Fluid Simulation Overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/fluid-simulation-in-unreal-engine---overview)). The research found no shipped game that runs Niagara 3D gas live. A forum responder asked which game the poster had in mind **[Community]** ([Epic forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/low-performance-with-cached-gas-simulations-niagara-fluids-3d-gas-sim/2177751)), and even the optimistic studio blog was "not aware of a shipped game" using it **[Low-reliability]** ([StraySpark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/niagara-fluids-for-gameplay-beyond-vfx-2026)). Nothing suggests the solver algorithm has changed since 5.0-5.3. Later work went into rendering and caching **[Inference]**. Epic offers no neural fluid feature. Its Neural Network Engine is a general inference API **[Epic]** ([NNE overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/neural-network-engine-overview-in-unreal-engine)). Our 3D screen-space neural detail only sharpened the image anyway, so we have no neural lead to claim in 3D **[Ours]**.

## A head-to-head test is worth doing if it stays narrow

The test is worth doing because every performance comparison above rests on mismatched or unsourced numbers. One controlled run would replace them. It should answer three questions: what Niagara costs at our resolution, what it costs at the resolution it needs to look good, and whether our smoke at about 2 ms looks as good as Niagara's at its own cost. It should not try to compare integration features, where the answer is already known.

Concretely, run on the same idle RTX 5080 at 2560x1440, in a Standalone or packaged build rather than the editor, as Epic staff advise for profiling **[Epic staff]** ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/working-with-niagara-fluids-to-create-water-simulations)). Use one buoyant plume and one fire with matched box size, emitter, buoyancy, dissipation and camera. Profile Niagara at Resolution Max Axis **64, 128 and 200** with ProfileGPU, recording the simulation stages (and the pressure stage on its own) separately from the render pass. Run it twice, once with the template ray-march and once through Heterogeneous Volumes with indirect lighting and scene shadows off, so the renderer does roughly what ours does. Record Pressure Solve Iterations and VRAM for each case. Run 1, 4 and 8 simultaneous instances, since neither side has published multi-effect costs. Then capture fixed-camera frame sequences from both systems and score them with the same metrics against our steered 256^3 reference, plus a blind A/B preference test by a few people, scoring fire separately from smoke. If possible, read back a Niagara velocity grid and compute its divergence residual. That would confirm or refute the inferred solver gap directly.

Report our pipeline honestly alongside it: the Python sandbox numbers, and the D3D12 branch's numbers if it can be made to run the same scene. Engine overhead for us is still unmeasured. The test should take days, not weeks, and its result decides the strategy. If Niagara at 64 costs about what we do and looks no worse, our solver lead is academic and the work should go into integration. If Niagara needs 200 and about 6+ ms to match our 2 ms, we have a measured case for live 3D smoke in games.

## Conclusion

The comparison changes what our lead is. It is not the Eulerian method, which Niagara shares. It is the allocation: a strong solve on few cells, plus cheap transported detail, inside a budget Epic does not claim to hit for live 3D gas. That lead is fragile in two ways. It has never been measured against Niagara, and the costs we have not yet paid (scene compositing, real lights, mesh collision, several effects at once) are exactly the ones that made Niagara's renderer expensive. Expect our 2 ms to grow once those are added.

The strategic opening is real but narrow. Epic steers games to bake, its renderer is still Experimental, and nobody appears to ship live 3D gas. A cheap, good-looking live effect would therefore stand alone in the market rather than compete with Niagara head-on, but only if it can be made to work inside an engine. The head-to-head test measures the first half of that claim. A minimal engine integration with mesh collision and one game light would test the second half, and it is the larger remaining risk.
